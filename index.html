The bottleneck isn't goods. It's logistics.

European hospitals, wholesalers and port companies regularly write off usable surplus stock. Meanwhile, aid organisations with a concrete need lack the means to pay for transport, which often costs more than the goods themselves.

Freight capacity, customs clearance, coordination with the right receiving party and funding for the journey: that link is where aid gets stuck. It's the only part we take on.

One point of contact for both sides

Companies with surplus rarely ship to a crisis zone themselves: it takes time, money and knowledge of sanctions and export rules. Aid organisations often can't fund the transport. We take over exactly that middle piece.

Five steps, every shipment

We never take ownership of the goods or responsibility for the final delivery. We arrange and fund transport from point A to point B, and document every step.

  1. Intake

    A company tells us it has surplus stock or spare capacity.

  2. Verify & classify

    We check the goods are permitted, not sanctioned and not dual-use, and that transport is feasible.

  3. Match

    We link the offer to a current, verified need from a recognised channel.

  4. Ship & fund

    Freight space, customs documents and costs are arranged, covered by donations or sponsorship.

  5. Confirm

    Arrival at the accredited partner is confirmed and recorded, as the basis for reporting to donors.

Specialists in the missing link

We focus on the logistics between supply and demand, so medical aid ends up where it's needed most.

What we do

  • Map surplus stock and spare capacity at Dutch companies
  • Match it to verified demand from recognised aid organisations
  • Fund freight, customs and storage through donations and sponsorship
  • Coordinate and document both sides of every shipment

What we don't do

  • Raise money for food, medicine or goods themselves
  • Verify end recipients ourselves; we only work with accredited mechanisms
  • Deliver directly to beneficiaries; our role stops at a partner's cargo door

Why we start with one pilot

Aid organisations receive many well-meant offers that don't match what is actually needed. We work the other way round: we start from a verified need reported by an accredited aid organisation, only send equipment that has been tested and proven to work, check every shipment against sanctions and export rules in advance, and only ship once the transport is paid for.

With one carefully documented pilot, we want to show that this approach works. We follow the shipment from collection to arrival and record every step: the checks, the costs and the lead times. Only once the first shipment has demonstrably arrived do we scale up.

We call it a success when

  • The shipment demonstrably arrives at the intended, accredited recipient
  • The process is documented and repeatable: checklist, contacts, lead times
  • At least one port partner wants to support the next shipment

We never start a shipment without the transport costs covered in advance.

The matchmaker model already works elsewhere

We're new, but the model isn't. Two international organisations show what it can do at scale.

97.9%

Airlink

Pools available air freight capacity and surplus stock for verified aid organisations, without owning a fleet. In 2024, 97.9% of its $13.1 million revenue came from contributions.

117

MedShare

Redistributes surplus medical supplies to healthcare facilities in 117 countries. Receiving facilities order exactly what they need, so donated equipment doesn't go unused.
